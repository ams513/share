Stark Warden 2.0 lets people rehearse calls with debt collectors and scammers before the real one

An offline tool from software company Stark adds call practice, a plain-language rights guide and letter drafts for people in the United States facing abusive collection or scam pressure. Nothing typed in it leaves the device.

2026-11-20. Stark today released Stark Warden 2.0, a $10 browser-based tool for people who are being pressured by debt collectors or scammers. Warden 2.0 lets a person practise an escalating collector call with a coach, read ten federal debt-collection rules in plain words with their sources, and fill in dispute, validation and stop-contact letters from a short interview. It runs offline, needs no account, and stores everything only on the user's own device.

Stark's founder built Warden after watching people be hurt by abusive debt collectors and scammers. Callers who say there is a warrant, that an employer will be told, or that a debt must be paid within the hour with gift cards rely on fear and speed. Warden is meant to slow the moment down and give the person on the other end of the call the words, the rules and the paperwork.

What is new in 2.0

  • "I'm being pressured right now" mode. One calm screen with five things to say, five things not to do, a script to end the call, permission to hang up, and a breathing guide.
  • Collector call practice. Four role-play calls that escalate the way real pressure does: urgency, guilt, a request to "verify" a Social Security number, a fake $50 deal, threats of jail and of calling an employer. After each reply a coach shows a calm, lawful answer and the rule behind it, with its source. Users can type their own words and see the coach react.
  • Your rights in 60 seconds. Ten federal rules for debt collectors in plain language, each citing its source (the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, the CFPB's Regulation F, or the FTC) and the date it was written.
  • "What happened?" interview. Seven short questions fill in a dispute and validation letter, a stop-contact letter, and complaint drafts for the CFPB, the FTC and a state attorney general, then set out a plan in order.
  • Is this even real? Checks what a collector sent or said against the validation-notice requirements and highlights red flags such as gift-card payment, arrest threats or "we can't mail anything."
  • Violation timeline. A contact log becomes a printable evidence packet with calls per week and the patterns that may break call-limit and call-time rules.
  • Medical debt, lawsuits and scams. Letters for itemized bills and financial assistance, an answer-deadline estimate for people who are sued, a scam message checker, and a library of common scam patterns (fake IRS, fake debt relief, utility shut-off, grandparent emergency, crypto romance, fake collectors, recovery scams).
  • Family screens. A simple screen with three large buttons, a trusted-person card with a family code word, and four printable fridge cards.
  • Spanish. The welcome screen, the pressure screen, the rights guide and the family screens are available in Spanish. This is a machine translation pending review and is marked as such on every Spanish screen.

Warden 1.0 tools remain, including a link inspector, 14 letter builders, a statute-of-limitations checker, zombie-debt and re-aging checks, an identity-theft recovery plan and an encrypted vault.

Privacy

Warden has no accounts, ads or analytics, and the page blocks network connections. Everything a user enters is saved only in their browser on their own device, and the vault is encrypted with their passphrase.

Limits, stated plainly

Stark Warden is general information, not legal advice. It is not a law firm, a credit counselor or a government agency, and using it does not create a lawyer-client relationship. Rules vary by state and by a person's facts, and Warden points users to legal aid and consumer-protection attorneys. No outcome is guaranteed: the tool cannot promise that a debt will go away or that a scam will be caught, and it cannot prove whether a caller or message is real. Its legal information was written from federal rules as known on November 20, 2026. It covers the United States only.
